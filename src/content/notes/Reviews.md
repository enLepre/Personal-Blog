I did my PhD in materials science from 2019 to 2023. I remember when ChatGPT was announced. I remember trying it after a few weeks of hearing everyone talk about it, finding it unhelpful, and dropping it to focus on my real research. At the time, my biggest struggle was literature review. I worked on carbon materials for catalysis and by that time the field was so saturated that critical papers like [guano-doped graphene](https://pubs.acs.org/ancac3/article-abstract/14/1/21/602927/Will-Any-Crap-We-Put-into-Graphene-Increase-Its?redirectedFrom=fulltext) started to appear, criticizing the overproduction of scientific reports without a clear contribution to knowledge.

I kept telling myself that experimental work did not seem to be the rate-determining step in science. Finding, reading, and organizing literature was the real deal, and getting a comprehensive grasp of the field felt almost impossible. Reviews were supposed to help with that, but often reviews felt more like a self-promotion campaign for the authors than a comprehensive overview of the field. With more than ten relevant papers appearing every day, even the best review would become outdated within a few years.

Compared to those times, frontier large language models have made terrific progress and over the course of my postdoc alone, they went from inventing literature entries to providing a full perspective on a field. If I have a scientific question, I never open Google Scholar or Web of Science; I go straight to Claude or ChatGPT. Often their analysis will come out too wordy and difficult to read, but if I scroll through the citation list, I get the relevant papers I was looking for. So what is the point of a literature review in 2026?

> *Review are there to take responsibility for the words generated by an LLM*

Indeed, while they can perform amazingly on tasks like literature review, LLMs cannot take responsibility if mistakes are made. However, we are still human and if mistakes are made, we need to find a cause. “Hallucinations” are not a good enough excuse and we need the human in the loop. My bet for the future job market is the same: knowledge workers will increasingly focus on quality assurance rather than knowledge production.
