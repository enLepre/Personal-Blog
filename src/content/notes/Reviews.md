I did my PhD in materials science from 2019 to 2023. I remember when ChatGPT was announced. I remember trying it after a few weeks of hearing everyone talk about it, finding it unhelpful, and dropping it to focus on my real research. At the time, my biggest struggle was searching and synthesizing the literature. I worked on carbon materials for catalysis and by that time the field was so saturated that critical papers like [guano-doped graphene](https://pubs.acs.org/ancac3/article-abstract/14/1/21/602927/Will-Any-Crap-We-Put-into-Graphene-Increase-Its?redirectedFrom=fulltext) started to appear, criticizing the overproduction of scientific reports without a clear contribution to knowledge.

I kept telling myself that experimental work did not seem to be the rate-determining step in science. Finding, reading, and organizing literature was the real deal, and getting a comprehensive grasp of the field felt almost impossible. Review articles were supposed to help with that, but often reviews felt more like a self-promotion campaign for the authors than a comprehensive overview of the field. With more than ten relevant papers appearing every day, even the best review would become outdated within a few years.

Since my PhD, frontier large language models have made terrific progress. Over the course of my postdoc alone, I went from seeing them invent references to using them to help me build an overview of a field. If I have a scientific question, I never open Google Scholar or Web of Science; I go straight to Claude or ChatGPT. Often their analysis will come out too wordy and difficult to read, but if I scroll through the citation list, I often find papers relevant to my question. LLMs have solved one of my biggest problem in doing research and the same reviews were trying to address. So what is the point of a review article in 2026?

> *Reviews are there to take responsibility for the words generated by an LLM.*

Indeed, while I find them useful for searching and synthesizing the literature, LLMs cannot take responsibility if mistakes are made. However, when mistakes are made, we still need someone to blame. “The model hallucinated” is not a satisfying answer: we want someone we can hold accountable. I suspect this shift extends beyond scientific publishing. Knowledge workers are increasingly spending their time evaluating and interpreting AI-generated work. However, I bet their value will not decrease, but shift to taking responsibility for the result rather than generating it.
