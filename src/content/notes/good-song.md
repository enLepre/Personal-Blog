I love music. I like listening to my Kid A vinyl as much as I love cranking up Taylor Swift on Spotify. There is hardly any genre I dislike, given the right context. Sometimes, the right context is just time passing by, music ageing, and perspectives changing.What I’ve always found fascinating about people who love music is their strong feelings about songs, artists, or genres—and I expected to share those feelings. A rap fan would shit on trap music using the same words that a rock fanatic would have used about rap music a few years earlier. I have always asked myself: why? What drives those strong feelings? What makes a good song good?

Music needs context as much as any other form of art. Listening to an Avicii song at a party where everyone is excited and wants a good beat to dance to is AMAZING. Bring the same song to an early-morning yoga retreat as background music for breakfast, and you will make even the calmest person lose their mind.Yet context is not just a matter of the time of day. Context can be induced. For example, seeing KISS live already tells you what to expect: something as shocking as their elaborate makeup. Yet shocking for whom? In the 1970s, such music might have been a blast, yet it sounds almost like pop in the context of today’s music and its evolution over fifty years. That’s another example of context: the time frame. “Sabato” is a song by Lorenzo Jovanotti that I hated. I found it cheap and boring, with lyrics no one could relate to—so much so that I felt insulted by it. However, about five years after it had been in the charts, I listened to an acoustic version of the same song, and it hit me like a truck. It sounded so cool and intimate, yet energetic and realistic. I loved it so much that I had to revisit Jovanotti’s music, to the point that he became my favourite artist.

I still ask myself what made that acoustic cover change my perspective so much. I initially thought it might have been the fact that the version was acoustic. So I dropped my love of electric guitars and effects in favour of acoustic guitars and more natural sounds.This approach led me to listen to every song in terms of its core components: CHORDS. However, I soon came to a realisation: all songs use the same chords, and if you have found a new progression, it is only because you haven’t listened to enough songs. So why do certain songs hit harder?Because one cannot find a single silver bullet when multiple variables are interacting. A song does not become beautiful because it uses a certain chord progression, a particular melodic structure, or visual effects. It does because those elements interact with each other and with our perspective to evoke emotions that would otherwise remain hidden.

> *The best wine is the first one to be finished at the table*

Wine tasting is no different from music tasting. It requires knowledge of what to look for. It involves the interaction of multiple factors, such as colour, smell, and taste. Wine then interacts with your food and, as a host, you might be surprised to find that the most expensive bottle of wine you placed on the table has been left full. Indeed, one can hardly predict what the future of a song will be. Educated guesses might be made, but who checks for the biases in that education? Music is like wine, and wine is like any other product. No one knows what is going to be useful, and only taking a risk will allow you to discover it. Yet, among all the parameters, only time can tell.  The best songs are the ones that survive in the memory of a generation. The best technologies are those that allow that generation to reproduce. One could study prior attempts, but if a formula existed, someone else would already have applied it. What we are left with are only our attempts and the freedom to pursue them.
