Organizations allow individuals to combine their efforts to address problems too large to be solved alone. However, coordinating these efforts is not a trivial task, and some individuals specialize in directing the work of others towards common goals. Among the many challenges involved, evaluating whether a problem is actually being solved is perhaps the most difficult. When problems require particularly long periods to be addressed, evaluations tend to focus on individual stages rather than the bigger picture. Consequently, individuals begin optimizing their performance within these smaller stages, sometimes at the expense of the overall objective.

> When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.

This is [Goodhart's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goodhart%27s_law), which describes how metrics can become unreliable when they are used as targets to evaluate the performance of individuals or organizations. It is a troubling concept for those who believe that quantification is the key to solving problems, because it exposes the limitations of measurement itself. Yet, looking at the development of academic performance metrics, it becomes remarkably clear how this phenomenon operates. For example, focusing excessively on the h-index or journal impact factors can discourage long-term research, favoring short, incremental projects that produce a larger number of smaller publications. Researchers are consequently encouraged to prioritize projects that generate measurable results within the time frames of their evaluations. Activities such as teaching, mentoring, and developing skills beyond a narrow research specialization may receive less attention, not because they are less valuable, but because their contribution is more difficult to quantify. Scientific output thus risks becoming disconnected from the broader objectives of research institutions, potentially limiting their contribution to scientific progress and society.

The challenge lies in the variety of contributions a researcher can make. We currently reward innovation, but scientific rigor, teaching, and the conservation of knowledge are equally important. Fundamental research can be extremely valuable even when its applications are neither obvious nor immediate. Indeed, some of the most important scientific advances emerged from questions that initially appeared to have little practical relevance. Nevertheless, the uncertainty of future applications cannot justify making innovation the only objective of academic institutions. How important is the synthesis of a new catalyst for the oxygen evolution reaction when there are no jobs available for researchers trained in that field? What would a person with a good heart value more: an innovation with no foreseeable application, or the teaching of established knowledge that helps someone secure a stable job and contribute to the economy? The answer cannot be the same for every researcher or institution. Yet, in pursuing ambitious projects and impressive headlines, institutional policies risk turning one particular measure of academic success into a universal objective, creating the very problem described by Goodhart's law.

The solution must therefore be found within the problem: broadening our definition of success rather than simply multiplying the metrics used to measure it. Evaluating the h-index alongside teaching performance, mentoring, or employment outcomes might provide a more complete picture, but each of these indicators can become a target and suffer from the same limitations. No combination of numbers can fully capture the diversity of contributions made by researchers and institutions. Evaluation should therefore combine quantitative indicators with qualitative judgment, while regularly reconsidering whether the chosen measures still reflect their original objectives. More importantly, not every institution should pursue the same goal of publishing in *Nature*. Some may prioritize fundamental discoveries, while others may focus more strongly on education, applied research, or the needs of their local economies. In turn, local industries should communicate to universities the professional skills they expect to need as they grow. By allowing different institutions to pursue different objectives, and evaluating them accordingly, we may create an environment in which a person with a good heart can succeed without having to exploit the system.
