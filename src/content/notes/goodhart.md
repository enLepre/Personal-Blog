Organizations allow individuals to combine their efforts to address problems too large to be solved alone. However, coordinating these efforts is not trivial. Among the challenges, evaluating whether a problem is actually being solved is perhaps the most difficult. When problems require long periods to be addressed, evaluations tend to focus on individual stages rather than the bigger picture. Consequently, individuals begin optimizing their performance within these smaller stages, sometimes at the expense of the overall objective.

> When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.

This is [Goodhart's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goodhart%27s_law), describing how metrics become unreliable when used as targets. It is a troubling concept for those who believe quantification is the key to solving problems, but academic performance metrics offer a clear example. Focusing excessively on the h-index or journal impact factors can favor short, incremental projects over long-term research. Researchers are encouraged to prioritize measurable outputs, while teaching, mentoring, and broader scientific objectives receive less attention. Consequently, scientific output risks becoming disconnected from the needs of society, potentially limiting its contribution to scientific and technological progress.

Yet, evaluators themselves respond to similar incentives. I feel that teachers have been grading students more and more generously. Even for a PhD at a top institution it is rare to see someone fail their own students. Those educators respond to the students’ need to have a certain type of certification to access the next career steps and they do not want to be held responsible for holding back their future. However, when too many people with equal qualifications but different sets of skills access the market, the same qualification loses its value. Then, it is reasonable for the market to become more and more difficult to access for new professionals who need months before finding a suitable position, leaving us worse off than before.

How to make the world a better place is a good heart problem. Yet, the road to hell is paved with good intentions. Goodhart's law teaches us that even when trying to be as quantitative as possible, the results might leave us negatively surprised. What we are left with is therefore only the recognition of these processes and our ability to spot a bad choice and change it. This iteration is as frustrating as it is useful but it is the winning principle of the scientific method and what brought us from sticks and stones to flying into space. Yet, we should always ask ourselves: if it were that easy, why wasn't it done before?
