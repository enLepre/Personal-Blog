Organizations allow individuals to combine their efforts to address problems too large to be solved alone. However, coordinating these efforts is not trivial, and some individuals specialize in directing the work of others towards common goals. Among the challenges involved, evaluating whether a problem is actually being solved is perhaps the most difficult. When problems require long periods to be addressed, evaluations tend to focus on individual stages rather than the bigger picture. Consequently, individuals begin optimizing their performance within these smaller stages, sometimes at the expense of the overall objective.

> When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.

This is [Goodhart's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goodhart%27s_law), describing how metrics become unreliable when used as targets. It is a troubling concept for those who believe quantification is the key to solving problems, but academic performance metrics offer a clear example. Focusing excessively on the h-index or journal impact factors can favor short, incremental projects over long-term research. Researchers are encouraged to prioritize measurable outputs, while teaching, mentoring, and broader scientific objectives receive less attention. Consequently, scientific output risks becoming disconnected from the needs of society, potentially limiting its contribution to scientific and technological progress.

The challenge lies in the variety of contributions a researcher can make. We reward innovation, but scientific rigor, teaching, and the conservation of knowledge are equally important. Fundamental research remains valuable even without foreseeable applications, but innovation cannot be the only objective of academic institutions. How important is synthesizing a new catalyst for the oxygen evolution reaction when there are no jobs available for researchers trained in that field? What would a person with a good heart value more: an innovation with no foreseeable application, or teaching established knowledge that helps someone secure a stable job? In pursuing ambitious projects and impressive headlines, institutions risk turning one measure of academic success into a universal objective.
